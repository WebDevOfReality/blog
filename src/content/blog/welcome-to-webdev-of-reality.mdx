### “Who the hell is this guy, and what makes him an expert?!”

Short answer is **I’m not!**  Definitely not an expert but I have been building software for over a decade. My name is Anthony Renteria, I am a Senior Software Engineer at [Olo](https://www.olo.com/). Olo has been (what my computer science professor, Dr. Beard would call) a trial-by-fire 🔥. Olo provides restaurants the ability to provide their customers with online ordering. Before DoorDash, Ubereats and even the iPhone, there was [text-based mobile ordering provided my Olo!](https://fortune.com/2026/09/01/online-restaurant-ordering-ecommerce-marketplaces/)

“What could be so complicated about ordering food” is what I thought when was first going through the technical interview rounds for Olo. 7 years later know my answers. **Everything.** I have learned so much from the being in the 🔥. Knowledge absorption from some of the most brilliant engineers I have ever met. I have also learned so much from doing things the wrong way. Can you really be an “expert”  if you haven’t broke ordering in production for 1000s of customers? 😬 

As mentioned earlier I am a certified Tinkerer. AI has reinvigorated that love for technology and innovation. Over the last year and a half I have been working with AI to figure out how to best utilize it for my work. I was a skeptic in those earlier days. “It’s just a bullshit generator” but then again so am I. It was once I started experimenting with workflows and agent orchestration, I saw real benefits. I created a workflow called “The council of Elrond” (yes I name most of my tools after pop culture). 

AI agent Elrond’s job was to analyze the “dossier” I prepared for him, determine from the pool of subagents we had built, who was best suited to investigate the task. The council would convene and investigate. Each council member investigated from a different angle based on its agentic directives. Then they would come together, discuss findings, and create a plan based on what they found. The council would have disagreements and bring the other councils members in to help resolve disagreements. The final report would give list of where the council agreed, also where they disagreed and why. I used this for a spike (technical investigation) ticket. It came back with a very detailed report in under 10 minutes. The report noted a disagreement with  the docs agent (whose knowledge was rooted in Olo documentation) and the back-ended agent (rooted in the Olo codebase). Documents said one thing but the actually code behaved differently. Something I would not have caught, that could have left the implementer of the ticket spinning their wheels because I made an assumption.
